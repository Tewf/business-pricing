# Against

The evidence against what the front page assumes, and the disagreements. Sources are in [review](review.md). None is deleted to make the subject look stronger.

**Shape:** [against](https://github.com/Tewf/reader-ergonomics-review/blob/c43f2770000a9af5c2aa3cbc6f997da7a7e13b34/shapes/against.md)

## Against "only a costly act counts as evidence of payment"

- An auction, a costly form, did not reduce the bias much against a referendum vote (Penn 2018, abstract, secondary). Measured.
- Open-ended and conjoint answers were biased yet might still give the right demand curve and the right price (Miller 2011a, abstract, secondary). Measured.
- A steak study found the same for a quality change (Lusk 2004, abstract, secondary). Measured.
- The meter, hypothetical and biased, gave results comparable to BDM (Kloss 2016, abstract, secondary). Supported with caveat.
- So a paid measure is the benchmark for the level of a price, and a stated one may be enough to rank prices. The review does not claim more.

## Against "the premium for self-design is large"

- Every bid in the best evidence is from business students in a classroom or a laboratory, on goods of about 100 euros or less (Franke 2010, Studies 2 to 5, read). Measured.
- In Study 2 the designers reproduced a given design, which the authors say limits the effect (Franke 2010, Study 2, read). Measured.
- Study 5 compares two levels of freedom and has no off-the-shelf arm (Franke 2010, Table 6, read). Measured.
- The effect needs a finished task (Norton 2012, abstract, secondary). Measured.
- The 100% increment on watches is read from an abstract and its sample was not checked (Franke 2004, abstract, secondary). Measured.

## Against "Van Westendorp's range is a price"

- The meter's points have little reasoning behind their names, a vendor says (Sawtooth 2024 Westendorp, page, read). Practice.
- It is biased by its hypothetical nature (Kloss 2016, abstract, secondary). Supported with caveat.
- Its origin is a 1976 paper that was not found online (Van Westendorp 1976, located only).

## Disagreements

| Point | One side | Other side |
|---|---|---|
| Do indirect methods measure WTP better than direct ones? | indirect methods are assumed more accurate, a prevalent assumption in marketing (Schmidt 2020, abstract, secondary) | indirect methods overstated the real value more (Schmidt 2020, abstract, secondary) |
| Do auctions reduce the bias? | a choice-based elicitation reduced it (Murphy 2005, abstract, read) | an auction reduced it little against a referendum (Penn 2018, abstract, secondary) |
| How big is the bias? | 21% on average for consumer goods (Schmidt 2020, abstract, secondary) | a median ratio of 1.35 and a mean of 2.60 (Murphy 2005, Table VI, read) |
| Is personalisation bad for consumers? | total consumer surplus fell (Dube 2023, abstract, secondary) | over 60% of consumers gained (Dube 2023, abstract, secondary) |

- The size of the bias may split by the goods: Schmidt 2020 is on consumer goods, and Murphy 2005 mixes public goods, which that paper links to more bias.
- A choice-based mechanism and an auction may differ in the stakes and the subjects, which the confounding in Murphy 2005 prevents us from separating.

## Against the sources themselves

- Eighteen of the thirty formal sources were read from the abstract, and eight were located only. No figure marked secondary was checked in the paper.
- The premium rests on one research group's studies, with students and small samples (Franke 2010, Franke 2014).
- The practice sources are vendors and a library, each about its own product; three score below 10 of 20 (sources).
- A preprint is not among them, but a working paper stands for Murphy 2005 and Armstrong 2000, so a published figure may differ.
